The state’s problem is weighted set packing

Under the 20% rule a state doesn’t submit counties — it submits groups of counties, and it chooses the groups. Formally, that choice is a weighted set-packing problem.

Fix an application and a data window. Each county i has a 24-month labor force Li, an unemployment count Ui, and a value Vi to the policymaker of getting it waived (its ABAWD caseload, say). With national rate α˜, the operative threshold is

α=min{1.2×α˜,10%}

(the min is the paper’s modeling of the statutory 10% rule as a cap — see the rules tab for the legal definition, which is looser). A group g is feasible if it is geographically contiguous and its aggregate rate clears the threshold:

∑i∈gUi ∑i∈gLi ≥α ⇔ ∑i∈gsi≥0, where si=Ui−αLi

The slack form is the useful one: each county contributes a fixed surplus or deficit si, and a group works iff the surpluses cover the deficits. A small county far above the threshold can carry a much larger neighbor sitting just below it — which is exactly what states do (AR FY2008). The state’s problem: choose pairwise-disjoint, contiguous, feasible groups to maximize total covered value ∑g∑i∈gVi. Choosing a maximum-weight collection of disjoint sets from a menu of admissible sets is weighted set packing — NP-complete in general.

A natural heuristic: greedy

Enumerate the admissible groups, then repeatedly commit the highest-value group that is disjoint from everything committed so far, until none remains. Greedy is fast and often good, but it carries no optimality guarantee: an early greedy pick can consume a county that two other groups each needed, stranding value a patient planner would have kept. (The paper itself analyzes the packing problem directly rather than prescribing greedy — the heuristic here is expository.) The demo below is rigged so you can beat it.
