Two ways into a waiver

Since PRWORA (1996), a state can waive the SNAP ABAWD time limit for an area that satisfies one of two statutory prongs (7 U.S.C. 2015(o)(4)(A)): the area “has an unemployment rate of over 10 percent,” or it “does not have a sufficient number of jobs to provide employment for the individuals.”

The first prong is a concrete numeric test. The second — insufficient jobs — is an umbrella that FNS operationalized in guidance into concrete routes: the Labor Surplus Area designation, the Extended Benefits trigger, a state-computed “20% above national” test, and a set of softer criteria. The project labels every waived area with one of the canonical criterion codes below; these are the same codes and colors used on the map.

The three thresholds people conflate

Three different numeric objects circulate under names like “the 10% rule” and “the 20% rule.” They are not the same test, and the LSA formula’s internal 10% is a different 10% from the statutory rule.

The 10% rule (statutory)

A level test: the area’s unemployment rate is over 10%. The regulation (7 CFR 273.24(f)(2)(i)) accepts three evidentiary forms — a recent 12-month average, a recent 3-month average, or an historical seasonal rate — so it is not a single fixed-window statistic. Its practical edge is timing: an area with a 12-month average over 10% is self-certifiable, meaning the state may begin operating the waiver at submission rather than waiting for FNS approval.

The 20% rule (state-computed)

The workhorse of the corpus: the area’s 24-month average unemployment rate is at least 20% above the national average over the same 24 months — equivalently, area rate≥1.2×national rate. Computed by the state from BLS LAUS (not seasonally adjusted), compared against the BLS national series. It has no floor and no ceiling: in a strong national labor market the operative cutoff can fall well below 6%, and the map is full of areas waived at rates a casual reader would call low.

Provenance note: the working paper dates this criterion to the August 2006 guidance; the primary sources show it was codified in the 2001 final rule (66 FR 4437) and merely restated in 2006. The KB carries this as a pending correction to the paper.

Labor Surplus Area (DOL designation)

An annual DOL/ETA designation, not an FNS computation. Its core is the same 20%-above-national idea — 20 CFR 654.5 designates areas whose two-calendar-year average unemployment rate is “120 percent of the national average… or 10 percent or higher,” but never below a 6.0% floor. That internal 10% is an auto-qualify ceiling in the LSA formula, not the statutory 10% rule. LSA also runs over a different geography — DOL “civil jurisdictions” (cities ≥ 25k, counties, balances of counties, New England towns) — so a unit can be an LSA without passing the state-computed 20% test, and vice versa. For waiver purposes a current-FY LSA area is “readily approvable” and self-certifiable.

Extended Benefits trigger

When DOL determines a state qualifies for Extended Benefits (insured-unemployment or total-unemployment-rate triggers “ON”; EUC eligibility in 2008–2013), FNS grants an automatic statewide waiver of up to 12 months. In the paper’s model this is a bypass: no allocation problem is left for the state to solve.

Federal suspensions

ARRA suspended the time limit nationwide from April 1, 2009 to September 30, 2010; FFCRA did the same from April 1, 2020 to June 30, 2023. No application was needed. (The end of pandemic Emergency Allotments in February 2023 is a different provision — the ABAWD suspension ran to June 30, 2023.)

Softer criteria

The insufficient-jobs umbrella also admits qualitative evidence: a low and declining employment-to-population ratio, declining occupations or industries, or an academic study. The ETP route was eventually quantified (2021 guide): ETP at least 1 percentage point below the national average and declining over a reference period of at least 4 years, complemented by a 24-month unemployment rate at least 10% above national. Few waivers in the corpus rest on these criteria alone.

Regulatory timeline

  • 1996PRWORA creates the time limit and the two-prong waiver authority; first FNS guidance (Dec 1996): LSA, EB, >10%, softer criteria — no standalone 20% test.
  • 2001Final rule codifies 7 CFR 273.24(f), including the freestanding 20%-above-national criterion and the ETA-tied window rule.
  • 2006FNS guidance restates the 20% test; mandates non-seasonally-adjusted BLS data; standardizes rounding and no-double-counting (memo never located — see below).
  • 2016Guide adds the contiguity / economic-region clarification and the academic-study criterion.
  • 2019Rule adds a 6% floor and restricts grouping — vacated in 2020, formally rescinded 2021; §273.24 reverts to the 2001 text.
  • 2021September guide restates the reverted criteria (the version in force for the late corpus).
  • 2025OBBB strikes the insufficient-jobs prong; only the 10% rule and the AK/HI 1.5× test survive.

Which months of data may be used

A 20%-rule application at date t does not get to pick any 24 months it likes. The window menu is anchored to the DOL LSA calendar.

“The 24-month period can begin no earlier than the period the Department of Labor uses to calculate LSAs for the Federal fiscal year in which the waiver is implemented… a waiver that would start in Fiscal Year 2021 could be supported with a 24-month period beginning any time after (but not before) January 1, 2018.” — FNS waiver guide, Sep 2021 (verbatim; identical in the 2016 guide)

Equivalently: for a waiver starting in fiscal year FY, the window may begin no earlier than January 1 of calendar year (FY − 3), and the state may use any consecutive 24-month window on or after that January for which data exist. The guides’ own lookup table:

Waiver FYWindow may begin no earlier than
2021Jan 1, 2018
2022Jan 1, 2019
2023Jan 1, 2020
2024Jan 1, 2021

Two wrinkles worth knowing. First, the working paper glosses this rule as “January of two fiscal years prior,” which read literally is about a year more restrictive than the verbatim guide language; the KB treats the guide as controlling (pending correction #2). Second, the 10% rule has its own recency constraint rather than a window anchor: the 12- or 3-month average must be recent — for a January 1, 2018 start, the furthest permissible 12-month look-back is February 2016 – January 2017.

The anchor plus recency creates a real degree of freedom: by choosing a start date in September (current FY) versus October (next FY), and by applying later as new BLS months post, a state can expand its menu of admissible 24-month windows by roughly a dozen. Each window carries its own national threshold, so window choice is itself part of the optimization the set-packing tab describes.

BLS vintages

The unemployment rate an application cites is not a fixed fact — it is a vintage: the estimate BLS published as of some date, later revised.

As general background (this paragraph is standard BLS practice, not a rule from the project KB): LAUS county estimates are published monthly with a short lag — a preliminary figure that is revised the following month — and then re-benchmarked in the annual processing round each spring, which can revise several years of history at once. The working paper’s data section notes the practical corollary: a month-t release carries preliminary county figures for t−3 and revised figures for t−4, and the annual January-data release revises many past years. So the rate a state saw when it applied can differ from the rate in today’s LAUS file — which is why the paper restricts to post-2011 application years, the earliest for which BLS-archived vintages exist.

unverified — primary source not located  Which vintage a state may legally rely on at application date t is nowhere pinned down in the sources the project has retrieved: the KB records no verified vintage-selection rule. What the corpus shows instead is FNS exercising judgment case by case — 49 unit-rows in the extraction carry an explicit older-vintage flag.

Rounding

unverified — primary source not located  The 2006 guidance reportedly standardized an aggregation-and-rounding procedure, but the August 2006 memo has never been located, and the paper does not reproduce the rule. What follows is the de facto convention inferred from the corpus — and the corpus shows FNS applying it inconsistently.

The convention that fits most documents: compute the group’s aggregate rate (total unemployed over total labor force across the 24 months), round both the area rate and the 1.2×national threshold to one decimal place, then compare. Because a rounded 6.0 can stand for anything from 5.95, rounding is outcome-determinative at the margin — and the margin is exactly where states aim (see the set-packing tab).

FNS states the national 24-month average as 6.8% and the 20%-above threshold as 8.1% — but 6.8×1.2=8.16. Both NC bundles come in at 8.05%, so both qualify only because FNS truncated rather than rounded the threshold.

Case file · AL FY2000 open in map →

The refusal direction again: Dallas County prints at 9.95%; the state rounded to “10 percent” for the statutory rule. FNS rejected the round-up — and noted that the statute requires a rate over 10%, so a flat 10% would fail even if the rounding stood.

Half-up in Louisiana, truncation in North Carolina, refusal-to-round in Alabama: the convention is real enough to decide cases and loose enough that any replication has to pick a rule and flag exact-equality cases. If the sign of the discrepancies holds up, a systematically permissive rounding practice is a finding in its own right.

Grouping areas

States do not have to qualify areas one at a time — they may define the areas. This is the rule that turns an application into a combinatorial problem.

“USDA will give States broad discretion in defining areas that best reflect the labor market prospects of program participants and State administrative needs… Accordingly, states should consider areas within, or combinations of, counties, cities, and towns…” — FNS guidance, Dec 1996

Three operative rules, in the project’s reading:

  • A group qualifies on its aggregate. The combined unemployed over the combined labor force must clear the threshold; individual members need not qualify on their own. A high-rate seed can carry lower-rate neighbors.
  • Contiguity (or an “economic region”). unverified — primary source not located  The 2016 guide is credited with requiring groups to be geographically contiguous or part of an economic region; the regulation itself only says states may define areas. Empirically the corpus respects contiguity almost everywhere — with catches like a “four contiguous counties” group in KY FY2016 that is disconnected as drawn.
  • No double-counting (2006 guidance, same unlocated memo): no unit may appear in more than one waived group within an application — groups are disjoint.

Case file · AR FY2008 open in map →

The grouping choice made explicit on one page: 42 counties qualified individually, each on its own rate — plus 31 more counties as a single joint bundle in which every member is below the threshold (own rates 3.7–5.4 against 5.5), carried entirely by a combined 5.6. The state qualified everything it could individually, then bundled the exact residual that could only pass jointly.

Worked example: one application, three criteria

Tennessee’s FY2005 application shows the whole toolkit in a single FNS response: 22 groups — 12 under the 20% rule, 9 under LSA designation, and 1 under the statutory 10% rule — mixing areas that qualify on their own rates with joint bundles whose members only pass in aggregate.

Read the table the way FNS read the application: the LSA rows ride on a DOL designation (no rate arithmetic to check); the per-unit 20%-rule rows each clear the threshold alone; the joint bundles are where the grouping rule earns its keep — several counties none of which qualifies individually, packed so the aggregate lands at or just above the cutoff. The single 10%-rule row is the statutory prong making a cameo where one area’s rate cleared 10% outright.
